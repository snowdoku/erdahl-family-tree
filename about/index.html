How to read this site

What the grades mean

Every person has one grade. It is the research team's judgement of the evidence, using the rules in the project's research standards.

Confirmed
A primary record that names this person and this relationship was read: a church book, census, civil registration, probate, or gravestone photograph, or a faithful index of one.
Probable
A strong circumstantial fit with nothing contradicting it, but no single record states it outright.
Speculative
A plausible candidate only. Kept so the next researcher can test it; do not rely on it.
From family
Told to us by the family and not checked against a record.
Living
A living relative: details are withheld.

For most people the grade is on the link to the child through whom they are an ancestor: a man can be certainly real and certainly the right age, yet only Probable as the father of the next person down. The page says so in its "How sure are we?" section when that is the case.

How a person's page is laid out

Every page has the same sections in the same order. A section with nothing in it is left out.

  1. At a glance: birth, death, burial and marriages. The date as the record gives it is shown in small type beside the reading form.
  2. Family: parents, husbands or wives with the children they had together, brothers and sisters. Tap any card to go to that person.
  3. Timeline: the dated events, in order. Tap one to jump to the paragraph that describes it.
  4. Life story: what the records say about their life, in date order.
  5. How sure are we?: the grade and why; the argument for the identification; records that disagree; corrections; other candidates that were considered and set aside; searches that found nothing; and the open questions with the record that would settle them.
  6. Sources: every record cited, with a link where one exists.
  7. Research log, folded away: notes about method, and which research batch wrote each paragraph.

Paragraph numbers (¶12) are the order in which the research notes were written. A note that mixes two kinds of statement is split, and its parts are numbered ¶12a, ¶12b.

Names, dates and places

Names are written as the records write them. Before about 1900 Norwegians had no fixed surname: Kari Torjusdatter is Kari, Torjus's daughter, and Jon Tronsen Sætnes is Jon, son of Tron, of the farm Sætnes. Names are not rearranged to look like modern surnames.

Dates: "about", "before", "after" and "between" are kept exactly as the record or the estimate has them.

Places run from the smallest to the largest, country last.
